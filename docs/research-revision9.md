# Research behind the revision 9 tutoring trials

Reviewed 2026-11-12. The practical question was how to pick the next move when a learner has already specified a goal, gives an ambiguous answer, or wants a fresh problem. This is a targeted evidence review. It includes primary experiments, an observational study, a replication and two research syntheses. The populations and comparisons matter, especially where recall of words and troubleshooting a circuit gave different results.

## What revision 8 and Jane Street already do

At PPP commit `364188ab9a20d9e53e469b1dfef32b04c3eaf6db`, the tutor already used known goals and aids, asked questions that could distinguish two explanations, and prepared separate challenge keys. Its six initial held-out conversations tied the prior version on all 18 scored criteria. Two targeted and two reserve cases also tied. In development, the tutor correctly keyed a turning point yet offered the learner only “increasing” or “decreasing” at the instant of zero rate. This is a reason to inspect the *delivered question* before reviewing its answer key. Earlier PPP provenance also distinguishes creator observations, borrowed design ideas and experimental results. The complete Jane Street source at upstream HEAD `7c6715c29548a3b39d4a9545c698ee4fd17a7213` was the same version previously inspected by PPP. It exempts one-off questions, starts a requested course with three to five placement questions, asks for predictions or practical outputs, and allows a direct answer on request. Its learner can request a pace change and carry a summary to another session. We retain the move of asking for evidence that could change a judgment, including a trace or counterexample. PPP lets a supplied attempt or a relevant first task serve as the diagnostic and permits an immediate worked step. There is no learner outcome comparison of the two skills here. ## Findings and their instructional reach

**Setting a goal for difficult work.** In a laboratory class-scheduling task, 96 participants needed to learn strategies as they worked. Seijts and Latham found that a specific learning goal improved performance over a general instruction to do one's best. A specific, difficult outcome goal performed worse than that general instruction. Adding intermediate outcome goals did not improve performance over the longer-term goal alone. An experiment that assigned goals says little about the ideal length of a tutoring intake. Our inference is narrower. If a learner already wants to diagnose why a counter skips a state, that decision supplies the first task. An arbitrary target score adds no information about which trace they need to interpret. [Seijts and Latham, 2001](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/job.70)

**Deliberate practice and difficulty.** Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer linked skill-group differences among violin students to retrospective estimates of focused practice. Macnamara and Maitra's preregistered replication found an association but did not reproduce a difference at every skill level. Both studies depend on recollections of practice. Neither tested a threshold for advancing in mathematics. Give a practice session an identifiable target, feedback and another opportunity to perform it. Record the conditions of an observed success. Minutes, confidence and one good answer cannot calibrate the next problem's difficulty on their own. [Original study](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1993-ericsson.pdf) · [Replication and results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31598236/)

**Retrieval, confidence and transfer.** University students learning foreign-language vocabulary benefited from repeated retrieval after their first correct recall. Their predictions failed to track their eventual performance. In Butler's prose experiments, repeated testing also improved performance on some new inference questions a week later. Pan and Rickard's synthesis of 122 experiments reported an average standardized transfer effect of 0.40 relative to additional exposure, with weaker evidence for transfer to problems involving worked examples. Its estimates depend on the match between initial and final tasks, and some estimates shrank after adjustment for publication bias. Record a confidence judgment as self-report. To check transfer, preserve an unseen decision and specify what changes. To check retention, revisit a taught idea after a recorded interval. A new setting and a delay can be evaluated separately. [Karpicke and Roediger, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18276894/) · [Butler, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20804289/) · [Pan and Rickard, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29733621/)

**Worked examples, cognitive load and fading assistance.** Forty Dutch university students with little recent science preparation learned to troubleshoot faults in parallel circuits. Four worked examples and alternating examples with problems produced statistically indistinguishable immediate scores. At one week the example-only group scored higher. A different set of field and laboratory experiments with school and college learners found that removing worked steps progressively helped on *near* transfer. Far transfer was less clear. In mechanical trade apprentices, the relative advantage of studying examples changed with increasing experience. The instructional inference is to work the step the learner cannot yet coordinate, then hand back a meaningful decision. When the learner can already justify that step independently, change one constraint. These studies supply no way to infer cognitive load or choose an optimal difficulty from a short chat response. [Circuit experiment, methods and Table 1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cogs.12002) · [Renkl et al., 2002](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00220970209599510) · [Kalyuga et al., 2001, authors' abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ640537)

**How long to withhold help.** In two experiments on multiple-choice questions about prose, giving corrective information after a delay improved later cued recall relative to immediate correction. In a think-aloud study of ten introductory physics undergraduates using Andes, asking for help after negative feedback often preceded learning or recall, while repeated unaided attempts often failed to resolve the error. The first study manipulated timing on memory questions. The second observed self-selected help-seeking during a multi-step task, including difficulty with software conventions. It cannot identify a causal hint policy. For a cold assessment, a tutor can preserve the answer until the attempt is over. During instruction, a learner who identifies the missing relation can receive it immediately. These findings supply no fixed quota of wrong attempts. [Butler, Karpicke and Roediger, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18194050/) · [Ranganathan, VanLehn and Van de Sande, pp. 326–329 and discussion](https://rptel.apsce.net/index.php/RPTEL/article/download/2014-09019/215)

**Oral reasoning and fair feedback.** In two high-school geometry classroom experiments, students who used a tutor to identify the principle behind solution steps later gave better explanations and solved more transfer problems. They selected principles in structured software. Neither speech polish nor an adversarial exam was the intervention. In a different population, Abedi and Lord compared mathematics word problems with versions that reduced linguistic complexity. Among 1,174 eighth graders, the revised wording modestly raised scores overall. The changes mattered more in some groups. This study changed *question wording*. Our proposed extension to grading is to accept an equation, trace, diagram or plain-language explanation that demonstrates the target. If oral communication itself is the target, specify which aspect will be assessed. For ambiguous reasoning, reserve judgment and ask a short question that would change the next explanation. Silence leaves the reason unknown. [Aleven and Koedinger, experiments 1 and 2](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/koedinger/pubs/Aleven%20%26%20Koedinger%2002.pdf) · [Abedi and Lord, 2001](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S15324818AME1403_2)

**What a generated problem can measure.** The National Research Council's assessment framework connects a claim about competence, an observable performance and a rule for interpreting that performance. Its balance-scale example uses several response patterns to discriminate between plausible rules. This is an assessment-design framework, with no trial of LLM-generated problems. A practical application is to name the decision being tested, solve the exact learner-facing item independently and assign credit to evidence that would distinguish sound reasoning from a lucky answer. If the goal is selecting a method, inspect the title, subparts, visible rubric and hints for cues that select it. If multiple answers meet the stated assumptions, accept them or revise the item before release. [National Research Council, chapter 2 and boxes 2-1 through 2-3](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10019/chapter/4)

**Performance with and without a tutor.** Bastani and colleagues randomized high-school math classes in one Turkish school to ordinary GPT-4 assistance, a teacher-informed tutor, or the comparison resources. Across roughly 1,000 students, both AI groups scored higher during practice. On the subsequent unaided exam the ordinary interface lowered scores by 0.054 on a 0–1 scale relative to the comparison group. The teacher-informed tutor's estimated difference was −0.004 and statistically indistinguishable from zero. The study used closely matched problems in the same session. In a separate randomized experiment with 194 university physics students, Kestin and colleagues found higher post-test performance after a custom AI lesson than after an active-learning class. The comparison lessons, populations and tutor designs differ. Neither trial estimates long-term retention for PPP. Score what happens when the learner works independently, record prior exposure, and leave immediate assisted success attributed to the assistance received. [Bastani et al., especially Table 1](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) · [Kestin et al., study design and outcomes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6)

**Adapting the amount of worked geometry.** Salden and colleagues used a learner's earlier explanations of a particular angle theorem to decide whether to show a worked step or leave it to the learner. In a German high-school laboratory study, the adaptive group scored higher on immediate and one-week transfer tests in a planned comparison against the two other groups combined. A ninth-grade vocational-school trial gave a different result. The planned comparison found no immediate advantage. At three weeks the adaptive group outperformed standard tutored problem solving in a separate comparison that excluded fixed fading. The classroom data did not establish a benefit over fixed fading. For PPP, an unaided explanation of a theorem is a reason to leave a step using that theorem open next time. Handling a different theorem remains an empirical question. [Salden et al., 2009, experiments 1 and 2](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2008.01011.x)

**Questioning in a live tutoring dialogue.** Chi and colleagues recorded eleven college tutors teaching eighth graders about circulation. The tutors returned a week later to teach different eighth graders, this time with instructions to invite explanations from students and limit their own explanations and feedback. The groups’ post-test scores showed no significant differences across four question categories. The prompted students contributed more of their own reasoning and used the reading material more. The small groups and fixed order leave open whether practice by the tutors or differences between the students affected the comparison. One application is to ask a learner to connect a particular observation to a cause and listen for that connection before choosing the next question. The study supplies no basis for grading verbal fluency or requiring a sequence of prompts before a requested explanation. [Chi et al., 2001, methods and Table 4](https://education.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/image3_2.pdf)

## Proposed changes and ways to reject them

These are design hypotheses. The benchmark protocol owns the frozen cases, rubrics and numerical gates. A source supporting a teaching practice cannot establish that adding another instruction changes a model's replies.

| Candidate change | Predicted behavior and possible cost | A check capable of rejecting the change |
|---|---|---|
| Reuse the current goal and the evidence already available. | A defined goal leads to a feasible first task. A changed goal can reuse prior constraints that still apply. Starting immediately could silently choose the wrong application. | Present a supplied goal, an ambiguous goal, then a revised objective. Inspect what the learner can actually do on the next turn under the specified aids and time. Penalize redundant intake, stale constraints or an assumed preference stated as fact. Include a one-off answer request. |
| Choose feedback that distinguishes between plausible reasons for the learner's answer. | A terse sound argument receives credit. A correct result with invalid reasoning receives a specific correction. A learner who is stuck gets a usable next step. Repeated probing could withhold wanted help. | Compare two attempts with the same conclusion and different reasoning, one ambiguous response and an explicit solution request. Require the response to change with the evidence. A fabricated objection, verdict based on silence, or another question after an explicit stop fails. |
| Verify both the cold problem and the tutor's materials. | An unseen task preserves the decision while admitting a defensible solution under all stated conditions. Overzealous secrecy could omit a necessary specification. | Recalculate or enumerate from the learner-facing text alone. Try a boundary input, an impossible brief and an alternative valid method. Separately inspect the prompt for missing premises, method cues, valid choices, scoring totals and concealed transfer. Replace a published or previously viewed item before using it as unseen evidence. |

For a later learner trial, start with two comparable task pairs, a brief independent baseline, and a rubric fixed before instruction. Counterbalance the tutoring versions across pairs. Record actual time, hints, answer exposure and protocol deviations. Compare an immediate unaided solution and, at an agreed delay, a fresh problem that changes a decision. Have a scorer who did not teach the session assess de-identified work without seeing the version. Give the learner the requested solution at any time and record the exposure. With one learner, differences in task difficulty and carryover remain plausible explanations.
